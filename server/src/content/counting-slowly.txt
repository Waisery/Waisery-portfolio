Count slowly. It no longer takes 659 steps to get from the low wall beside the street to the fountain in the middle of the square. Twenty-five years have passed. She is no longer the girl who counted her steps to prove that her legs were growing, always impatient to become taller. Time has given her a remarkable grace. Even passersby who miss her striking face can be drawn to that elusive, elegant presence. She no longer runs headlong through the streets. Her heels strike the pavement with a clear, crisp sound, an instrument of confidence. From the moment she put on her first pair, bought with half a month's salary, she seemed to possess the courage to overcome anything. Later, in many ways, she did.

At first she strides freely along the road where traces of the old days can still be found. The town has changed, but she delights in every small resemblance, slipping back into the past through fragments of the familiar. A car horn breaks in, rebuking the pedestrian in its way. She stops abruptly, as if waking from a dream. The fountain is close now: 537 steps. Holding to some stubborn belief, she begins taking small, careful steps, trying to steal more time than a snail in a strange private ritual. When the count reaches exactly 659 at the fountain, she lets out a child's relieved breath. Then the journey seems already over. Sorrow is too pale a word. Something bursts in her, sending waves across an inner sea that will not grow still. A left turn would lead to the house where she once lived, though it has long since been demolished and rebuilt. Five unblemished years were sold for thirty thousand. A friend of her father's bought the country house and rebuilt it for his retirement. She cannot bear to look. She turns right instead.

There is the old house she knows so well, still surrounded by camellias. It is April; the garden is in bloom again. The distance from the fountain to Chen Shi's house is short. As a girl, it was not only running that made her heart race, but the face waiting there, his habitual smiling eyes, like the camellias flourishing beside him. Her cheeks would be flushed, her damp hair stuck to her forehead. Back then, this place was everything she understood by April in the human world. Nothing differs from her memory. That is what frightens her. She wants to turn away, but her legs are two heavy pieces of wood. She has no courage left.

“Xinci.”

It sounds like a murmur from a dream millions of years ago. The end of the name trembles with the pain of time and the dozen steps, or countless miles, between them. The feeling burns up into her head, like mustard swallowed by mistake, driving tears into her eyes. The world turns. In front of the person she once loved, she demands composure of herself. This is an apology.

They first met here too. She had just moved to the town. While her parents unpacked and sorted their belongings, she wandered without a destination. She did not like the place. Her mother's serious illness had brought them here on a doctor's advice, for the clean air. She felt she belonged elsewhere. She loved watching hurried people in the city: every day, dressed with care, they entered tall buildings and earned enough through their work to support themselves. Independent. They embodied a girl's idea of growing up.

She stopped before a house whose little yard was filled with flowers, crowding around the two-story building. The centers of the brilliant red blossoms reminded her of tiny, delicate wine cups. Camellias. She had seen only a single tree in a park before. They were as beautiful as the red dress in her mother's wardrobe, the one her mother had not worn since becoming ill. They seemed to belong to her mother's former life. Without thinking, she reached out to pick one.

“What are you doing?”

The sudden voice startled her into withdrawing her hand. A boy a head taller than her stood behind her with his arms folded. Chen Shi. When she turned, he pulled a fierce frown, as though he had smelled a rotten apple. It annoyed her. She immediately wanted to take him down a little.

“If you pick it, it will be dead in a couple of days.”

She planted her hands on her hips, lifted her chin, and answered with a sly light in her eyes. “It will die sooner or later anyway. It might as well see somewhere else. How dull to look at the same place your whole life.”

She had a talent for arguments. Seeing him at a loss, she could hardly keep her triumph off her face. At last Chen Shi scratched his head in surrender and carefully picked a camellia. Surprised, she reached to receive it, ready to thank him. Instead, he tucked it beside her ear. He laughed at her reddening face, delighted with his revenge, then became solemn. “It's a camellia. If you put it in water, it can live a few days longer. I'm Chen Shi. What's your name?” The sun seemed to have waited for that moment, spilling watery light into his dimples when he smiled. A thirteen-year-old girl's heart is easily moved. Sometimes a sentence is enough. Sometimes everything simply arrives at the right moment.

To Chen Shi, it all feels like a dream. He had stopped expecting to see Xu Xinci again. His days were a dull documentary with an audience score of 2.3. Occasionally he asked a friend who still knew her a roundabout question. Like a thief, he stole little pieces of news and fitted them together: the company she had once dreamed of joining, another promotion, the year she bought a home of her own. She had become someone remarkable. He had known she would, even then. Knowing did not give him control over his feelings. There were days when he felt unbearably desolate. And now, twenty-five years later, at 9:35 on an April morning, under a gentle sun, he sees a figure from behind who looks so much like her. Can life, that slow poison, produce beautiful hallucinations too? He has imagined her so often, time settling into intelligence and elegance. What makes him call out her name? After the instant of excitement comes pain. The distance is too great. His own life is too poor a thing. He will only make her sad, or uncomfortable. He begins to pray that she will pretend not to hear and walk away. Then he can tell himself it was not her. But the figure stops. He struggles for breath like a fish stranded on dry land. Time has begun to alter her beauty, yet a single look can still unsettle him as it did before. Compared with life, Xu Xinci is another kind of poison, one he has always taken willingly.

The frown that offended her at their first meeting was something he had forced onto his face. He loved the flowers his mother tended so carefully. Seeing someone reach for one, he had meant to scold the girl like an adult. Then she turned, and he forgot himself. He had never seen that face, yet she seemed like someone he had known for a long time. Xu Xinci. His racing heart told him, with a certainty that felt natural, that he liked her. Determined not to forget why he had spoken, he frowned harder. It only made her angry. The little girl stood as proudly as a peacock, her eyes bright, without the slightest thought of hiding it.

In fact, he had answered her argument in a small, involuntary voice: “But what if the world outside is too dangerous for the flower?” Hearing how childish it sounded, he immediately fell silent. He wanted her to think him mature and dependable.

“What did you just say?”

“Nothing.”

Following her reasoning, he picked a camellia. Such a lovely flower belonged beside her pale face. By the time he realized what he was doing, the unexpected deed was done. He could only hide behind laughter and think that she was beautiful even when angry. The April wind moved gently through everything. There was still an April in the human world.

Twenty-five years ago, the unspoken feeling between them was a thin mist neither could pierce, though both knew it was there. Twenty-five years later, the mist is gone. Everything that has changed is painfully clear. The remaining dew is made of awkward, sorrowful tears. Sunlight flashes from the diamond on Xu Xinci's middle finger and breaks the glass covering of his dream.

At thirty, Xu Xinci accepted a proposal from the celebrated writer Shen Qingyun. They met at a play.

“Two people, joined by one heart, performing their love story amid the confusion, noise, absurdity, and cruelty of life.” When the curtain fell, she thought she needed a tissue. Through her tears she met the eyes of the man beside her. “I think you might need this.” He was so much like Chen Shi, and yet he was different.

Her mother died in her sleep when Xinci was seventeen. There had been signs. They were forced to accept the fact calmly. After the cremation, she hid alone beneath her quilt and cried. Suddenly pebbles struck the window, several loud, frightening taps. She ran to look. Chen Shi stood below. Joy rose in her before she could help it. She slipped outside into the yard. Even the moon seemed to understand what neither of them said.

“What are you doing here?” Her voice was hoarse and sounded so unpleasant that she covered her mouth at once.

He smiled. “Because you would be sad.” His face was entirely serious.

Night hung low. Stars lay scattered everywhere. Carrying a flashlight, they walked a long road. There may have been a lament in the wind. On the hillside, holding Chen Shi's warm hand, she sat quietly. The secrets of death and love drifted together in the air.

Her father died when she was thirty-three. After the funeral, she sat silently on the steps outside the funeral home. In the night, Shen Qingyun drew her into his arms. Her tears disappeared without a sound into his black coat, striking at his heart. The late-autumn wind was bleak. One thing was certain: they loved each other. Xu Xinci was never careless with such things. She would not have been there unless she loved him.

Chen Shi knows this. At last he understands that the dream is over. The twenty-five-year dream began when he decided to stay in the town. Eighteen was a crossroads. Of course Xu Xinci would return to the place she felt was hers. Her examination results were good enough to take her to the city she wanted. Chen Shi hesitated. The city was not his home. He had no grand ambitions. His dreams involved Xu Xinci and the hometown he loved. His mother's serious illness made the choice for him. The sun that day was fierce enough to dry Xinci's tears. Neither said anything. They understood each other too well.

On the day he put her on the train, grief and love mingled inside him until they seemed like blood spilling over. The train pulled in with a high whistle. He waved, turned, and walked away. A great cry rose in his throat, but only a sob reached his lips. He looked back. The wind lifted the ends of her hair.

“Xinci, is April far away?”

…

“I'm sorry.”

“All right. I understand.”

…

“Even without protection, a flower can bravely embrace a new tomorrow on its own.”

Twenty-five years. They have both been moving forward, whether they wanted to or not.
